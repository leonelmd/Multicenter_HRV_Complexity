### Figure1
TITLE: Figure 1: Multicenter study design and demographic characteristics across international cohorts.
LEGEND:
**Overview of data sources and population statistics.** (A) Geographical distribution of the three participating centers: CETRAM (Santiago, Chile), Hospital Universitario Cruces (Bilbao, Spain), and Nagoya University Hospital (Nagoya, Japan), illustrating the cross-continental scope of the study. ### Figure2

### Figure3
TITLE: Figure 3: Circadian dynamics of cardiac autonomic complexity in the Nagoya cohort.
LEGEND:
**Circadian variation of physiological metrics across the full 24-hour Holter recording.** Analysis performed on the Nagoya dataset (minimum 4000 heartbeats per 4h window, 1hr sliding step). (A–D) Clock-aligned ensemble profiles of Heart Rate (A), rcMSE nAUC(1–20) Complexity Index (B), SDNN (C), and RMSSD (D). Solid lines represent the group mean smoothed over a 3-point rolling window; shaded bands show ± SEM. While Heart Rate shows a preserved circadian rhythm in both groups, The Complexity Index in controls (blue) exhibits a bimodal circadian profile, peaking in the early morning (02:00–08:00h) and late evening, consistent with nocturnal autonomic reorganisation. PD patients (red) show a persistently flattened profile that fails to achieve either the nocturnal peak or the evening recovery, reflecting the abolition of the normal circadian autonomic programme. (E) Discrimination power per 4-hour window: -log10(p) from independent t-tests, with the dashed line marking the significance threshold ($p = 0.05$). The 16–20h afternoon window shows the strongest group separation. (F) Boxplots comparing the Complexity Index across five key time windows (Night 00–04h, Morning 07–11h, Midday 11–15h, Afternoon 16–20h, Evening 20–24h). The afternoon window (starred) yields the largest and most consistent PD vs. HC separation.
END

### Figure4
TITLE: Figure 4: Cross-cohort validation of complexity loss using Multiscale Entropy (MSE).
LEGEND:
**Universal reduction of cardiac complexity in Parkinson's Disease.** Four recording contexts are analyzed: CETRAM (ECG, ~15 min rest), Cruces (PPG, 5–15 min), Nagoya 07–11h (ECG Holter, 4h morning block), and Nagoya 16–20h (ECG Holter, 4h afternoon block). (A–D) MSE curves displaying rcMSE Sample Entropy (y-axis) as a function of time scale $\tau$ (x-axis). For short recordings (CETRAM/Cruces), only scales 1–5 are statistically reliable (N/τ ≥ 200 rule). Error bars represent SEM. A consistent and significant reduction in entropy is observed for the PD group (red) vs. Controls (blue) across all centers and modalities, within the statistically reliable scale range (τ=1–5 for CETRAM and Cruces; τ=1–20 for Nagoya). (E–H) Violin plots of the HR-normalized Complexity Index (rcMSE nAUC / HR) for the corresponding recording context. Statistical separation between PD and Controls is confirmed at CETRAM and Nagoya (Mann-Whitney U, annotated p-values), demonstrating the biomarker's cross-cohort robustness.
END

### Figure5
TITLE: Figure 5: Diagnostic performance, machine learning validation, and feature independence.
LEGEND:
**Comprehensive benchmarking of handcrafted complexity features against standard HRV metrics and end-to-end deep learning.** (A–D) Individual AUC comparison across six biomarkers — Complexity Index (rcMSE nAUC/HR), DFA alpha1, SampEn (scale 1), SDNN, RMSSD, pNN50 — for each recording context: CETRAM (A), Cruces (B), Nagoya 07–11h (C), Nagoya 16–20h (D). Bars sorted by AUC; the Complexity Index ranks highest in Nagoya (both windows) and Cruces; in CETRAM, single-scale sample entropy (SampEn S1) marginally leads, consistent with the N/τ ≥ 200 constraint limiting short-recording complexity to the vagal-scale range where single-scale entropy operates equivalently. (E) Leave-One-Center-Out (LOCO) AUC per excluded center for three classifiers (Logistic Regression, RF, SVM). Dashed red line shows the 1D-ResNet deep learning reference AUC. (F) Random Forest feature importance from a pooled full-fit model; the HR-normalized Complexity Index ranks as the most discriminative of the seven features. (G) Direct comparison of the best handcrafted LOCO model vs. the 1D-ResNet per center. Handcrafted complexity features match or exceed end-to-end deep learning under center shift. (H) Multi-center LOCO ROC curves for the best-performing model. (I–K) Spearman correlation heatmaps showing inter-feature orthogonality for CETRAM (I), Cruces (J), and Nagoya 16–20h (K). The Complexity Index forms a distinct low-correlation cluster separate from linear time-domain metrics, confirming that it captures independent physiological information.
END

### Figure6
TITLE: Figure 6: Assessment of age-independency in cardiac complexity.
LEGEND:
**Decoupling pathological complexity loss from normal aging.** (A–D) Scatter plots of the Complexity Index (y-axis) versus Age (x-axis) for all four recording contexts (CETRAM, Cruces, Nagoya 07–11h, Nagoya 16–20h). Individual subjects are shown (blue: Control, red: PD) with linear regression fits and 95% confidence intervals (shaded). Pearson $r$ and p-values are annotated. No significant age-complexity relationship was detected in either PD or control groups across any cohort (all |ρ| ≤ 0.29, all p > 0.20). Despite the lack of within-group age trends — even in controls — the PD regression line is systematically displaced downward relative to controls across all four panels, indicating that the PD-related complexity deficit operates independently of any age effect and is not a manifestation of accelerated physiological ageing. The significant downward shift of the PD regression intercept indicates that the PD-related loss of complexity is additive to—and independent of—normal aging effects, supporting its utility as a disease-specific biomarker rather than a proxy for accelerated aging.
END

### Figure7
TITLE: Figure 7: Cardiac Complexity as a Novel Autonomic Biomarker — Physiological Interpretation.
LEGEND:

Positive correlations with vagal indices and negative correlations with HR and LF/HF reflect largely between-group covariation — PD patients have simultaneously reduced complexity and reduced vagal tone — rather than a within-person physiological dependency. (D) Incremental variance decomposition (McFadden R²): hierarchical logistic regression quantifying the unique predictive contribution of the Complexity Index beyond age, sex, and standard HRV (SDNN, pNN50). (E) Confound correction lollipop plot: raw vs. age/sex-adjusted Spearman ρ for all HRV–Complexity correlations (Pooled n=152), demonstrating that the complexity–autonomic relationship is not driven by demographic confounders. (F) Autonomic synthesis panel: raw ρ, partial ρ (age/sex-adjusted), and unique variance explained for each HRV metric across centers, providing a comprehensive map of the autonomic architecture of complexity. (G) Scale anatomy: ρ between MSE at each scale and key HRV metrics as a function of timescale for Nagoya (16–20h) and CETRAM. Diamond markers indicate scales with significant group differences (BH-FDR q < 0.05). Grey shading marks scales unreliable for short recordings.

### Figure8
TITLE: Figure 8: Clinical correlates of cardiac complexity in the CETRAM Parkinson's Disease cohort (PRELIMINARY — n=22/28).
LEGEND:
**Associations between rcMSE complexity and clinical severity in CETRAM PD patients.** Complexity index: rcMSE nAUC(1–5) / HR, computed from ~15-min resting ECG. All correlations are Spearman ρ with bootstrap 95% confidence intervals (n=1000 resamples); p-values corrected for multiple comparisons (Benjamini-Hochberg FDR). (A) Raw Spearman ρ heatmap: complexity vs. CISI-PD subscales (motor signs, disability, motor complications, cognitive status, total), Hoehn & Yahr stage, and disease duration. (G) Non-motor symptom analysis: Complexity Index in patients with vs. without each binary symptom (depression, constipation, urinary dysfunction, orthostatic hypotension, sleep disorders, REM behaviour disorder, olfactory dysfunction). Mann-Whitney U; * q < 0.05, ** q < 0.01. NOTE: This figure is preliminary (n=22/28); 6 subjects have MSE data but no clinical assessment on record.

### Appendix2
TITLE: Supplementary Figure 2: Per-scale discriminative power of MSE SampEn across recording modalities.
LEGEND:
**ECG vs PPG modality effect on MSE scale-1 entropy.** AUC (Controls vs PD) computed separately for each MSE timescale τ (Mann-Whitney U, two-sided) across all four recording contexts: CETRAM (ECG, ~15 min), Cruces (PPG, 5–15 min), Nagoya 07–11h (ECG Holter, 4 h), and Nagoya 16–20h (ECG Holter, 4 h). Solid lines and filled areas: scales within the reliable range (N/τ ≥ 200). Dashed lines and grey shading: scales beyond the reliable range for short recordings. Significance markers: * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001. In ECG recordings (CETRAM, Nagoya), scale τ = 1 captures true beat-to-beat RR interval variability and yields the largest group separation (CETRAM S1 AUC = 0.72, p = 0.002). In the PPG cohort (Cruces), inter-beat intervals are derived from photoplethysmographic waveform peaks: their detection introduces smoothing that compresses short-timescale variability in all subjects equally, attenuating the group difference at τ = 1 (AUC = 0.54, p = 0.63). Scales τ = 3–4 in Cruces achieve AUC ≥ 0.65 and p < 0.05, as coarse-graining averages out the PPG-specific noise, revealing the underlying autonomic complexity loss. The consistent direction of effect across all scales (Controls > PD in all cohorts) confirms that complexity reduction in PD is a real physiological signal, partially obscured at τ = 1 by PPG modality constraints. This figure motivates the borderline significance of the HR-normalized Complexity Index in Cruces and supports reporting this cohort as a consistent trend rather than a null result.
END
