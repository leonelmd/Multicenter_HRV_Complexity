## Fig. 1 — `Fig1_overview`

Overview of the multicentre design. Top: the three contributing cohorts, namely, Santiago (Chile), Bilbao (Spain) and Nagoya (Japan), together with the acquisition modality of each, i.e., resting electrocardiography, finger photoplethysmography acquired during functional magnetic resonance imaging, and a 24-h wearable chest-strap recording, respectively. Bottom: the analytical contrast drawn in this work, in which the interbeat-interval series is summarized either by A) conventional linear variability indices, or by B) multiscale entropy, whereby the series is progressively coarse-grained and sample entropy is evaluated at each scale, thereby characterizing the organization of the fluctuations across temporal scales rather than their magnitude alone.

Signal archetypes and conventional HRV context by cohort. (A–C) Interbeat-interval traces of the analysed participants in each cohort, plotted faintly with the group mean overlaid (CETRAM, first 5 min; Cruces, whole record; Nagoya, the full 24-h record of the 47 participants analysed in the 18:00–22:00 window). (D–F) Mean heart rate by group (Nagoya: 18:00–22:00 window), shown as violin plots with individual participants overlaid; PD have higher rates in all three cohorts, significantly so only in Nagoya. ## Fig. 3 — `Fig3_curves_index`

(A–D) Mean rcMSE curves with standard-error bands by group, for CETRAM, Cruces, the Nagoya 07:00–11:00 contrast window and the pre-specified Nagoya 18:00–22:00 window; shading marks the scale range entering the complexity index for that cohort, as set by the $N/\tau\geq200$ reliability rule; values beyond it are shown for context only. In CETRAM and Nagoya entropy rises with scale over the designated range, in CETRAM only when the R apex is located correctly (Section ?); in Cruces it falls from $\tau=1$ to $\tau\approx3$–$4$ in both groups (Section ?). (E–H) Complexity index (nAUC) by group for the same four datasets, shown as box plots giving median and interquartile range with individual participants overlaid; the upper annotation gives the unadjusted Mann–Whitney $p$ value and the lower annotation the $p$ value and AUC with mean heart rate entered as a covariate, which is the appropriate adjustment (Section ? and Supplementary Fig. S1), with HC3 standard errors. CETRAM: $p=0.031$, HR-adjusted $p=0.031$, HR-adjusted AUC 0.646. Cruces: $p=0.057$, HR-adjusted $p=0.062$, HR-adjusted AUC 0.605. Nagoya 18:00–22:00: $p<0.001$, HR-adjusted $p<0.001$, HR-adjusted AUC 0.811. The Cruces contrast points in the same direction but does not reach significance.

## Fig. 4 — `Fig4_age`

Complexity index against age, by group, for each cohort and window, namely, CETRAM, Cruces, the Nagoya 07:00–11:00 contrast window and the pre-specified Nagoya 18:00–22:00 window. Lines are ordinary least-squares fits with 95\% confidence bands, and the annotations give the Spearman $\rho$ of the complexity index (nAUC) with age, computed within group, together with its $p$ value. Seven of the eight within-group correlations are not significant; the exception is the Nagoya 18:00–22:00 PD group ($\rho=-0.48$, $p=0.01$), which does not survive correction for the eight tests performed. The group differences reported elsewhere therefore do not appear generally attributable to the age difference between groups, although the heart-rate-adjusted models of Table ? are the more direct control.

## Fig. 5 — `Fig5_parameters`

Discrimination and split-half agreement across the two free parameters of sample entropy, i.e., the tolerance multiplier $k$ in $r=k \sigma_x$ and the embedding dimension $m$. (A–B) AUC and split-half agreement (both halves at the whole-series tolerance; Methods) against $k$, with $m=2$ drawn bold and $m=1$ and $m=3$ faint. Both are flat, in that across $k=0.125$–$0.50$ and $m=1$–$3$ the AUC moves by at most 0.061 in CETRAM, 0.077 in Cruces and 0.040 in Nagoya. Red rings mark settings at which the estimator is undefined for more than 1\% of a cohort. (C–D) AUC and split-half agreement against $m$ at $k=0.2$. Discrimination is nearly indifferent to $m$, whereas agreement falls at $m=3$ in the two short cohorts, because longer templates match more rarely, and is flat in $m$ in Nagoya, so that $m=2$ sits at or near the agreement maximum. (E–G) AUC over the full $(k,m)$ grid per cohort, with the adopted setting boxed and cells where the estimator is undefined for more than 1\% of subjects marked by a red cross. The low-$k$, high-$m$ corner fails badly, i.e., at $k=0.05$ and $m=3$ the estimator is undefined for 90.4\% of Cruces and 55.1\% of CETRAM participants, which is the reason $k$ is not pushed below 0.125. (H) Summary of the adopted values and of the range across the usable grid. Parameters were fixed a priori by convention and were not optimized on the outcome; the per-cohort optima at $m=2$ do not coincide in any case, lying at $k=0.125$, 0.175 and 0.175, respectively. All values are nAUC over scales 1–5, in Nagoya over the full 18:00–22:00 window, where $k=0.2$, $m=2$ reproduces the primary value at those scales (AUC 0.842). A fixed absolute tolerance was considered and rejected, since it correlates with the interval standard deviation at $\rho=+0.47$ to $+0.93$ and leaves CETRAM at or near chance, i.e., it measures amplitude rather than complexity.

## Fig. 6 — `Fig6_detector`

Where the beat mark falls, and what it does to the entropy curve (CETRAM). (A) Six seconds of resting single-lead ECG with the marks of the Brammer detector used in the analysis (blue) and of the NeuroKit2 implementation of Pan–Tompkins (orange). (B) Two beats of the same recording aligned on the Pan–Tompkins mark, which falls on the R apex in the first and 25 ms later, on the S wave, in the second. (C) Timing of every Pan–Tompkins mark relative to the nearest Brammer mark over the whole recording; the two modes correspond to the R apex and the S wave. (D) The RR-interval series of the same 60 beats from each detector; the alternating mark adds a beat-to-beat error to the Pan–Tompkins series. (E) Group-mean rcMSE curves at scales 1–5, with standard-error bands, in the analysed CETRAM sample (42 controls, 27 PD) for each detector. The error raises entropy at $\tau=1$ and averages out under coarse-graining, so that the curve falls with Pan–Tompkins marks and rises with Brammer marks, while the group contrast is preserved (AUC 0.687 and 0.655). Panels A–D show raw detector marks, before ectopic correction, from a control recording chosen because its Pan–Tompkins marks alternate between the two fiducial points (51\% fall 15–60 ms late, against about a third in the median recording); display filter 0.5–40 Hz with a 50-Hz notch, grid 40 ms by 0.1 mV.

## Fig. 7 — `Fig7_recording_length`

Recording length determines which HRV marker is applicable. All panels derive from the Nagoya cohort, which is the source cohort of Suzuki et al.\ [ref], so that the minimum-SDNN values shown are a reproduction of that published analysis in the same participants. No window, scale or parameter in this figure was selected on the group contrast. (A) Complexity index in consecutive non-overlapping 15-min windows across the day, as group mean with standard-error band by clock hour; the pre-specified 18:00–22:00 window is shaded. (B) Discrimination by clock hour for complexity and for SDNN, where coverage varies by hour because recording start times differed. (C) All 24 candidate four-hour windows, with filled symbols surviving Bonferroni correction across the 24 tests and both the previously selected 16:00–20:00 and the pre-specified 18:00–22:00 windows marked; the latter discriminates less well (0.815 versus 0.859), which is itself evidence that it was not selected on the outcome. (D) Discrimination of the minimum-across-day statistic against the length of the window over which that minimum is taken within the 24-h record, for both markers; the abscissa is window length and not recording duration, so that approximately 92 windows per participant remain available at the 15-min point. The shaded region marks window lengths at which $N/\tau<200$ and complexity is therefore not estimable. (E) The four distributional summaries used by Suzuki et al., namely, minimum, first decile, first quartile and median across the recording, computed for SDNN in 100-beat windows and for complexity in 15-min windows. (F) Cross-cohort comparison of minimum SDNN against complexity, in Nagoya, in the analysed CETRAM sample and in CETRAM after additionally excluding the three retained recordings with SDNN above 100 ms (Section ?). (G) Scale-resolved sample entropy to $\tau=60$ in the pre-specified window, plotted against physiological timescale and shaded by frequency band. (H) Discrimination against timescale, which peaks at $\tau=3$, i.e., approximately 2.5 s at the cohort mean interval. (I) Effect of window overlap, recomputed from the raw interval series on a sliding grid rather than by resampling the consecutive windows, and expressed relative to non-overlapping windows: raising overlap from 0\% to 90\% increases the number of windows per participant from 92 to 911 and lowers the median minimum monotonically, because the minimum over more draws is mechanically smaller, degrading the AUC of the minimum, whereas the AUC of the tenth percentile is unaffected (that of the median, not shown, changes by less than 0.01).

## Supplementary Fig. S2 — `FigS2_spectral`

Spectral characterization and the beat-indexed time base, computed on the analysed series (CETRAM 69, Cruces 52, Nagoya 47, 18:00–22:00). (A, B) Spectral exponent $\beta$ in the beat domain (median 1.44, 1.24 and 0.61); none of the cohorts is white noise. (C) The exponent is steeper when the series is indexed by time. (D) Entropy of the real series against $1/f^{\beta}$ noise matched on length, exponent and standard deviation. In the two short resting cohorts the real and matched curves are close, i.e., their entropy is largely accounted for by the power-law spectrum, whereas the 24-h series is far less entropic than its matched noise (median excess of the noise over $\tau=1$–10, 194\%), consistent with deterministic structure, such as circadian, postural and activity-related changes, that a stationary power law does not reproduce. There is no group difference in $\beta$ in any cohort ($p=0.21$–$0.99$): PD alters the amplitude of the complexity measure, not the spectral exponent. Because an interval series is indexed by beat rather than sampled at a fixed rate, coarse-graining at $\tau$ spans $\tau\times$ mean RR seconds and the physical timescale differs between groups wherever heart rate does.

## Supplementary Fig. S3 — `FigS3_cruces_context`

The Cruces cohort in the context of the source study. (a) Discrimination, with bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals, for the complete resting HRV metric set of Iniguez et al.\ [ref] recomputed in the same 52 participants, together with our complexity index (outlined). (b) The corresponding Mann–Whitney $p$ values on a logarithmic axis. RMSSD, SD1 and HF power attain nominal $p<0.05$, none after correction for the ten metrics, and NNiqr, which the source study selected as its primary metric on the basis of its heart–brain synchronization maps, is not among them. (c) For comparison, the clinical autonomic findings reported in that paper, plotted on their own axis; these values are quoted from the publication and were not recomputed here, since we did not have access to the autonomic-test or neuroimaging data. Collectively, the panels indicate that the PD group is clinically dysautonomic while its resting interbeat-interval series separates the groups only weakly, and that the complexity index behaves like the conventional measures in this cohort.

## Supplementary Fig. S4 — `FigS4_length`

Sensitivity of the complexity index to analysed recording length. Each participant's interval series is truncated to a common number of beats, so that changes reflect length rather than cohort composition; each cohort is restricted to the participants present at all of its ladder points (CETRAM 50, Cruces 26, Nagoya 42), and the dashed lines mark the median record length of each short cohort (Cruces 498, CETRAM 1115 beats). (A) Index against analysed length. (B) Between-subject SD of the index. (C) Discrimination. (D) Paired within-subject difference from the 4000-beat value in Nagoya. (E) Agreement of each length with the 4000-beat value. (F) Curve roughness. Discrimination rises with length in Nagoya, the only cohort whose recordings permit the full ladder; split-half reliability, each half with its own tolerance, is given in the main text.

## Supplementary Fig. S5 — `FigS5_ppg_scales`

Per-scale discrimination across acquisition contexts. In CETRAM, scale $\tau=1$ yields the largest group separation; in the Nagoya 18:00–22:00 window separation is nearly flat over $\tau=1$–6 (AUC 0.83–0.84, peak at $\tau=3$) and declines slowly thereafter. In the photoplethysmographic cohort, interbeat intervals are derived from the maximum upstroke slope of the pulse wave; separation is similar to that in CETRAM at $\tau=1$–$4$ (AUC 0.64–0.67) and disappears at $\tau=5$, the scale at which the Cruces entropy curves turn and at which its records reach the reliability limit (main text, Results, ``The photoplethysmographic cohort''). Shaded regions mark scales that fail the $N/\tau\geq200$ reliability rule.

## Supplementary Fig. S6 — `FigS6_loco`

Cross-cohort transfer of the group contrast, leave-one-cohort-out. Logistic regression models were trained on two cohorts and tested on the held-out third, with features standardized within cohort without reference to group labels (Methods). (a) AUC in the held-out cohort for heart rate alone, the complexity index with heart rate, and SDNN and RMSSD with heart rate; error bars are 95\% stratified bootstrap confidence intervals and the dashed line marks chance. With the complexity index, held-out AUC was 0.680 [0.550–0.805] in CETRAM, 0.688 [0.536–0.830] in Cruces and 0.863 [0.747–0.954] in Nagoya, against 0.615, 0.639 and 0.745 for heart rate alone; the gain over heart rate (0.049–0.117) has confidence intervals that include zero in each cohort. SDNN and RMSSD with heart rate did not transfer to CETRAM (0.504). (b) Held-out ROC curves for the complexity model. This analysis is a robustness check, not a diagnostic classifier.

## Supplementary Fig. S9 — `FigS9_traces`

Raw-trace review of the CETRAM recordings with SDNN above 100 ms. Each row shows six seconds of single-lead ECG with the analysis beat marks (left; grid 40 ms by 0.1 mV, display filter 0.5–40 Hz with a 50-Hz notch, scale bar 1 mV) and the RR-interval series of the whole recording with that window marked (right; intervals clipped to 300–2000 ms for display). The first two rows are references, namely, the control and PD recordings closest to their group's median complexity index in the analysed sample. Excluded (red): two recordings with frequent ectopic beats, wide in the PD recording and mostly narrow and premature, followed by a pause, in the control; and one PD recording that lacks a recognizable QRS complex for most of its first $\sim$5 min, shown at the return of the signal, the second PD recording excluded on this criterion being analogous with $\sim$2 min. Retained (blue): a PD recording with sinus rhythm and artefact in its first $\sim$2 min, during which the detector placed marks on noise, one control recording being analogous; and a control recording with clean sinus rhythm and slow drifts of heart rate, which account for its high SDNN. The review was carried out by a clinician co-author and was not blinded to group (Methods).
