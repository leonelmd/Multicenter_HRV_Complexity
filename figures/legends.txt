### Figure4
TITLE: Figure 4: Cross-cohort validation of complexity loss using Multiscale Entropy (MSE).
LEGEND:
**Universal reduction of cardiac complexity in Parkinson's Disease.** Four recording contexts are analyzed: CETRAM (ECG, ~15 min rest), Cruces (PPG, 5–15 min), Nagoya 07–11h (ECG Holter, 4h morning block), and Nagoya 16–20h (ECG Holter, 4h afternoon block). (A–D) MSE curves displaying rcMSE Sample Entropy (y-axis) as a function of time scale $\tau$ (x-axis). For short recordings (CETRAM/Cruces), only scales 1–5 are statistically reliable (N/τ ≥ 200 rule). Error bars represent SEM. A consistent and significant reduction in entropy is observed for the PD group (red) vs. Controls (blue) across all centers and modalities, within the statistically reliable scale range (τ=1–5 for CETRAM and Cruces; τ=1–20 for Nagoya). (E–H) Violin plots of the HR-normalized Complexity Index (rcMSE nAUC / HR) for the corresponding recording context. Statistical separation between PD and Controls is confirmed at CETRAM and Nagoya (Mann-Whitney U, annotated p-values), demonstrating the biomarker's cross-cohort robustness.
END

### Figure6
TITLE: Figure 6: Assessment of age-independency in cardiac complexity.
LEGEND:
**Decoupling pathological complexity loss from normal aging.** (A–D) Scatter plots of the Complexity Index (y-axis) versus Age (x-axis) for all four recording contexts (CETRAM, Cruces, Nagoya 07–11h, Nagoya 16–20h). Individual subjects are shown (blue: Control, red: PD) with linear regression fits and 95% confidence intervals (shaded). Pearson $r$ and p-values are annotated. No significant age-complexity relationship was detected in either PD or control groups across any cohort (all |ρ| ≤ 0.29, all p > 0.20). Despite the lack of within-group age trends — even in controls — the PD regression line is systematically displaced downward relative to controls across all four panels, indicating that the PD-related complexity deficit operates independently of any age effect and is not a manifestation of accelerated physiological ageing. The significant downward shift of the PD regression intercept indicates that the PD-related loss of complexity is additive to—and independent of—normal aging effects, supporting its utility as a disease-specific biomarker rather than a proxy for accelerated aging.
END

### Appendix2
TITLE: Supplementary Figure 2: Per-scale discriminative power of MSE SampEn across recording modalities.
LEGEND:
**ECG vs PPG modality effect on MSE scale-1 entropy.** AUC (Controls vs PD) computed separately for each MSE timescale τ (Mann-Whitney U, two-sided) across all four recording contexts: CETRAM (ECG, ~15 min), Cruces (PPG, 5–15 min), Nagoya 07–11h (ECG Holter, 4 h), and Nagoya 16–20h (ECG Holter, 4 h). Solid lines and filled areas: scales within the reliable range (N/τ ≥ 200). Dashed lines and grey shading: scales beyond the reliable range for short recordings. Significance markers: * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001. In ECG recordings (CETRAM, Nagoya), scale τ = 1 captures true beat-to-beat RR interval variability and yields the largest group separation (CETRAM S1 AUC = 0.72, p = 0.002). In the PPG cohort (Cruces), inter-beat intervals are derived from photoplethysmographic waveform peaks: their detection introduces smoothing that compresses short-timescale variability in all subjects equally, attenuating the group difference at τ = 1 (AUC = 0.54, p = 0.63). Scales τ = 3–4 in Cruces achieve AUC ≥ 0.65 and p < 0.05, as coarse-graining averages out the PPG-specific noise, revealing the underlying autonomic complexity loss. The consistent direction of effect across all scales (Controls > PD in all cohorts) confirms that complexity reduction in PD is a real physiological signal, partially obscured at τ = 1 by PPG modality constraints. This figure motivates the borderline significance of the HR-normalized Complexity Index in Cruces and supports reporting this cohort as a consistent trend rather than a null result.
END


### FigureValidation
TITLE: Supplementary Figure S1: Methodological validation of the rcMSE complexity index (CETRAM cohort).
LEGEND:
**Validation of the estimator, its parameters, and the choice of summary index.** All panels use the CETRAM cohort (43 Control, 30 PD) after bSQI quality gating, Kubios beat correction and the 300–2500 ms physiological guard. Throughout, rcMSE is computed on RR intervals with the tolerance $r$ **fixed** at $0.2\times$SD of the original series and never recomputed per scale, using the Wu et al. (2014) coarse-graining scheme; every complexity index is the trapezoidal nAUC of the canonical toolbox, $\mathrm{nAUC}=\mathrm{trapz}(1..n,\,\mathrm{curve})/n$. (A) Entropy-vs-scale curves for five synthetic reference processes (mean $\pm$ SD over 8 realisations, unit variance) with the CETRAM group means ($\pm$ SEM) overlaid. The estimator reproduces the canonical signatures: white noise decays steeply toward zero, 1/f noise and AR(1) remain high and flat, and the deterministic logistic map and near-periodic signal sit low. Both cohort curves lie in the correlated-stochastic region between 1/f and AR(1), and PD is displaced downward from Control by an approximately constant offset at every scale rather than differing in curve shape. (B) Sensitivity to spurious long RR intervals injected into real CETRAM recordings at increasing rates (12 subjects, nAUC(1–5) expressed relative to each subject's clean value; mean $\pm$ SD). Dashed vertical lines mark the artifact rates present before the physiological guard was applied (Control 0.10%, PD 0.42%). At the PD rate the index is already reduced to roughly half its clean value, and because the artifact rate was itself higher in PD this inflated the apparent group difference — the empirical justification for the guard. (C) Parameter sweep: AUC for Control vs PD discrimination across embedding dimension $m\in\{1,2,3\}$, fixed tolerance $r\in\{0.10,0.15,0.20,0.25\}\times$SD, and entropy type (sample vs fuzzy). AUC spans only 0.72–0.78 across all 24 configurations, so the result does not depend on the parameter choice. (D) Complexity-index selection: AUC with bootstrap 95% CI (400 resamples) for seven candidate summaries, unnormalized (grey) and divided by mean heart rate (purple); asterisks mark $p<0.05$ (Mann–Whitney U). AUC values below 0.5 indicate the reverse direction (PD > Control) — this is why the significant marker on LRS(1–20)/HR denotes a *reversed* association, not a discriminative one. nAUC(1–3), nAUC(1–5) and single-scale SampEn perform equivalently within overlapping intervals; discrimination declines as slower scales are added, and the fitted slope of the MSE curve (LRS) carries no discriminative information in either direction. HR normalisation improves AUC by 0.03–0.05 for every area-based index. (E) Scale-range sweep: AUC of nAUC(1–$k$) as the upper scale $k$ increases from 1 to 20, with bootstrap 95% bands. Discrimination peaks at $k=2$–3 and decreases monotonically thereafter, indicating that the group difference is concentrated at the fast, vagally mediated scales accessible in a 15-minute recording. (F) Effect of recording length, computed on the subset of subjects with at least 1000 beats (n=53) so the comparison is not confounded by subject inclusion; discrimination increases monotonically with the number of beats analysed, and the within-group correlation between index and beat count is negligible (Control $\rho=-0.14$, PD $\rho=-0.04$, both n.s.), supporting the use of each subject's full recording rather than a common truncation. (G) Heart-rate normalisation diagnostics. Left: the unnormalized index is statistically independent of mean HR ($\rho=-0.14$, p=0.25). Right: dividing by mean HR shifts the index's correlations with traditional HRV metrics by an amount that tracks each metric's own HR-dependence ($\rho=-0.95$), so HR-normalised correlations partly reflect the injected $1/$HR term. The normalised index is therefore used for group comparison and the unnormalized index for all correlation and mechanistic analyses.
END

### Figure3
TITLE: Figure 3: Temporal and multiscale structure of cardiac complexity in the Nagoya cohort.
LEGEND:
**Complexity across the 24-hour cycle and across timescales (24-h Holter ECG; 23 disease controls, 27 PD).** Complexity is the refined-composite multiscale entropy index rcMSE nAUC(1–5) normalised by mean heart rate, computed with a fixed tolerance $r = 0.2\times$SD of the original RR series. (A–D) Clock-aligned ensemble profiles from **consecutive 15-minute windows** across the full recording (3,667 windows; median 91 per subject), shown as group mean $\pm$ SEM per clock hour: complexity index (A), heart rate (B), SDNN (C), and a movement proxy given by the standard deviation of successive 1-minute mean RR intervals (D). Fifteen minutes is the shortest window supporting rcMSE at $\tau\leq5$ under the $N/\tau\geq200$ criterion, and matches the CETRAM recording length so that the identical index is computable in all three cohorts. Shaded regions mark 00:00–06:00 and 22:00–24:00. No window is selected on the basis of group separation anywhere in this figure. Complexity is reduced in PD at every hour of the day, while heart rate is elevated, replicating Suzuki et al. (2022); the group difference in the movement proxy (D) is consistent with the reduced standing time and increased lying time those authors measured by accelerometry in this same cohort, and is reported here as confirmatory rather than novel. (E) Scale-resolved sample entropy in the 16:00–20:00 window, plotted against physiological timescale ($\tau\times$ mean RR interval) for $\tau$ = 1–60. The 4-hour window contains approximately 14,000 beats and therefore supports scales up to $\tau\approx70$; background shading marks the sub-respiratory (<2.5 s), high-frequency/respiratory (2.5–6.7 s), low-frequency/baroreflex (6.7–25 s) and very-low-frequency (>25 s) bands. Entropy is lower in PD across the entire accessible range. (F) Discriminative AUC (Control > PD) as a function of timescale. **Discrimination is maximal at $\tau$ = 15, corresponding to approximately 12 s within the baroreflex band (AUC = 0.865), rather than at the fastest scales (AUC = 0.823 at $\tau$ = 1).** These timescales are inaccessible to short recordings — a 15-minute record reaches only $\tau\leq5$ ($\approx$4 s) under the same reliability criterion — so the localisation of the deficit to intermediate timescales is a capability specific to long ambulatory recordings, and one that no scalar HRV metric can provide. (G) Distributional summaries of the per-subject 15-minute complexity values across 24 h. Discrimination is concentrated entirely in the low tail (minimum AUC = 0.852, 10th percentile 0.852, median 0.797, maximum 0.676), mirroring the minimum-value approach of Suzuki et al. for SDNN. The across-day range is uninformative (AUC = 0.562). (H) Complexity minimum, median and maximum by group (Mann–Whitney U). The complexity *floor* separates the groups strongly (p < 0.001) whereas the *ceiling* separates them only weakly (p = 0.034), indicating that PD is characterised by a lower attainable minimum with a broadly comparable maximum rather than by a compressed circadian range.
END

### FigureAppendix3
TITLE: Supplementary Figure A3: Sensitivity of refined composite multiscale entropy to recording length.
LEGEND:
**Recording length, not cohort or signal modality, sets the precision of the complexity estimate.** Each subject's RR-interval series was truncated to a ladder of beat counts (250–4000) and rcMSE nAUC(1–5) recomputed at each, with the tolerance $r = 0.2\times$SD recomputed from each truncated segment. Within each cohort the analysis is restricted to subjects present at every one of that cohort's ladder points, so trends are not confounded by attrition (CETRAM n=53 up to 1000 beats; Cruces n=26 up to 500; Nagoya n=36 up to 4000). Dashed vertical lines mark the median beat count actually analysed in Cruces (498) and CETRAM (1088). Because the Nagoya recordings span the full ladder, truncating them provides an internal reference for what a shorter recording of the same physiological signal can achieve. (A) Mean nAUC(1–5) $\pm$ SEM against beats analysed. The point estimate is stable across a sixteen-fold change in length within every cohort; cohorts differ in absolute level for the reasons given in the Methods (the tolerance $r$ is set by each recording's own low-frequency content, and the nAUC normalisation carries a scale-count factor), so absolute values are not comparable between cohorts. (B) Between-subject standard deviation of the same estimate, with a $1/\sqrt{N}$ reference. Precision improves steadily with length: in Nagoya the SD falls from 0.351 at 250 beats to 0.194 at 4000. (C) Discriminative AUC (Control > PD) against length. **Nagoya truncated to Cruces' length achieves AUC 0.61, and truncated to CETRAM's length 0.73 — closely tracking what those cohorts achieve at their native lengths (0.66 and 0.70).** The apparent superiority of the Nagoya cohort is therefore largely a consequence of recording duration rather than of cohort, population or recording modality. For CETRAM, discrimination increases monotonically from 0.64 at 250 beats to 0.70 at 1000, supporting the use of each subject's full recording rather than a common truncation. (D) Paired within-subject difference between nAUC at each length and nAUC at 4000 beats (Nagoya), with error bars showing the standard error of the difference. All differences are small (+0.008 to +0.016) and none is significant (Wilcoxon signed-rank, all p > 0.19): **the estimator is essentially unbiased with respect to length, so there is no systematic length bias that could be corrected by extrapolation.** What a short recording costs is variance, not accuracy. (E) Agreement between the short-segment estimate and the 4000-beat value from the same subject: Spearman $\rho$ (purple, left axis) rises from 0.46 at 250 beats to 0.94 at 3000, while root-mean-square error (red, right axis) falls from 0.32 to 0.08. (F) Mean roughness of the individual entropy curves, defined as the mean absolute second difference across scales divided by the curve amplitude. Curve smoothness is governed by length in all three cohorts; the residual offset of Cruces above the ECG cohorts at matched length reflects intrinsic pulse-transit-time variability of photoplethysmography, and is not removed by sub-sample peak refinement (tested separately).
END
